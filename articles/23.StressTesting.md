# Stress Testing

A **stress test** (also called *rush-hour*, *overload*, or *scale* testing) evaluates how your system behaves when traffic rises **above normal operational load**—for example **+50% to +100% (`1.5x–2x`) or higher** during paydays, deadlines, or major promotions.

Only run stress tests after your [Smoke Test](21.SmokeTesting.md) and [Load Test](22.LoadTesting.md) pass cleanly, so you are not debugging normal-load bugs under heavy traffic.

---

## Stress Testing vs. Breakpoint Testing

Teams often mix up these two terms—keeping them separate makes test design much simpler:

| Test Type | Traffic Shape | Goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stress Test** *(this guide)* | Ramp up to a **known above-average target** (or stepped overload plateaus), hold, and ramp down | Verify stability, graceful degradation, and recovery under heavy rush-hour load |
| **[Breakpoint Test](25.BreakpointTesting.md)** | One continuous climb toward a very high client ceiling until `terminationRules` (or the planned duration) stop the run | Discover an **unknown** maximum capacity limit automatically |

---

## Pattern 1: Single Above-Peak Plateau (Recommended Default)

In most cases, a stress test uses the **exact same simple shape as a [Load Test](22.LoadTesting.md)**—**ramp-up → steady plateau → natural drain**—scaled up to a higher client count with a proportionally longer ramp-up.

**Example assumptions:** normal load is 100 clients, stress target is **200 clients (`2x` normal)**, clients ramp gradually over ~2 minutes (`arrivalDelay: 600` ms), and the overload plateau holds for 30 minutes (`duration: 1800` seconds). Expect higher latency than normal load, so thresholds are looser than an average-load test.

```yaml
name: AbovePeakStressTest
variables:
- name: targetUrl
  value: https://api.example.com/resource
rounds:
- name: HighLoadStressRound
  numberOfClients: 200
  arrivalDelay: 600
  iterations:
  - name: StressResource
    mode: D
    duration: 1800
    httpRequest:
      url: $targetUrl
      httpMethod: GET
    failureRules:
    - metric: "ErrorRate > 0.05"
      errorStatusCodes: ">= 500"
    - metric: "TotalTime.P95 > 2000"
    terminationRules:
    - metric: "ErrorRate > 0.20"
      errorStatusCodes: ">= 500"
      gracePeriod: "00:00:30"
    - metric: "TotalTime.P95 > 5000"
      gracePeriod: "00:00:30"
```

## Pattern 2: Step-Up Staircase with Recovery Check

When you want to observe **multiple overload levels in one continuous run** (`50 → 100 → 200` active clients) and verify that latency recovers as load steps back down (`200 → 150 → 100`), use additive `stages` with `startupDelay` plateaus inside a single round.

**Why one round instead of separate rounds:** separate rounds drop traffic to `0` between levels and can still start later rounds after an earlier iteration terminates. Keeping the staircase in **one round** escalates pressure continuously and lets `terminationRules` stop the entire test immediately if an early step melts down.

```yaml
name: StepUpStressTest
variables:
- name: targetUrl
  value: https://api.example.com/resource
rounds:
- name: StaircaseStressRound
  stages:
  - numberOfClients: 50
    arrivalDelay: 200
  - numberOfClients: 50
    arrivalDelay: 200
    startupDelay: 170000
  - numberOfClients: 100
    arrivalDelay: 100
    startupDelay: 170000
  iterations:
  - name: StressResource
    mode: D
    duration: 540
    httpRequest:
      url: $targetUrl
      httpMethod: GET
    failureRules:
    - metric: "ErrorRate > 0.05"
      errorStatusCodes: ">= 500"
    - metric: "TotalTime.P95 > 2000"
    terminationRules:
    - metric: "ErrorRate > 0.20"
      errorStatusCodes: ">= 500"
      gracePeriod: "00:00:30"
    - metric: "TotalTime.P95 > 5000"
      gracePeriod: "00:00:30"
```

**How the staircase behaves (`15` minutes total):**
- **`0:00–3:00`:** 50 active clients.
- **`3:00–6:00`:** +50 join → **100 active clients**.
- **`6:00–9:00`:** +100 join → **200 active clients** peak.
- **`9:00–15:00`:** Earlier waves complete their 540-second `duration`, stepping active concurrency down (`200 → 150 → 100 → 0`) so you can confirm clean recovery in the live dashboard or Grafana.

## What to Watch During a Stress Test

- **Graceful vs. catastrophic degradation:** Does latency rise predictably (or return clean `429` rate-limit responses), or does the service throw `500`s, exhaust DB connection pools, and crash?
- **Consistency on the plateau:** Response times under heavy load will be slower than average load, but they should stabilize on the plateau rather than climbing endlessly toward timeouts.
- **Post-stress recovery:** Once load ramps down, response times and error rates should return to normal without requiring a service restart.
